Llull. In the Fourth Figure the outer ring is fixed and the two inner rings turn; in medieval copies they were real paper discs tied through the centre with a knotted thread, which is the knot in the hub here. Each letter B–K stands for nine things at once, one per column of his Alphabet (only some are offered here). Llull kept triples of three different letters and ignored their order: 729 readings become 84 columns, each expanded into 20 "chambers" by reading each letter either in Figure A or in Figure T.

Turns and views. A turn is any time the wheel comes to rest in a new position. A turn is wasted if it shows nothing you haven't already seen. When the rings all have the same number of items, one position shows a whole family of readings at once (for Llull, the nine rotations of one triangle drawn across the rings), so the table splits into families and the fewest turns needed equals the number of families. For unequal rings the families overlap, and finding the true minimum is a set-cover problem; the page reports a greedy count, which is only an upper bound.

The other presets. Harsdörffer's Fünffacher Denckring der Teutschen Sprache (1651) put five rings of German word-parts on paper discs; the English rings here are an imitation, not his. The exquisite-corpse wheel swaps the Surrealists' folded paper for rotation. The English renderings of Llull's terms, and especially the "predicate" forms, are my own.
