BEATS & SHAPES

Two toys about chords. The Beat Lab shows why some notes sound smooth together and others rough: it is mostly physics you can see. The Chord Clock turns chords into shapes, and shows what turning and mirroring them reveals. Neither needs you to read music.

I. The Beat Lab

A musical note is not one frequency. It is a stack of sine waves called partials, at 1, 2, 3, 4… times the note's main frequency (its fundamental). Play two notes together and their partials overlap. Where a partial of one note lands exactly on a partial of the other, the two simply add up. Where two partials land close but not exactly together, they interfere: the loudness swells and fades, as many times per second as the difference between their frequencies. That is a beat. Slow beats sound like a wobble; fast ones blur into roughness.

  1. Press Play. You hear tone 1 (red) and tone 2 (blue). Tick tones 3 and 4 to make chords.
  2. Move tone 2 with its slider (the ‹ › buttons nudge it by one cent), pick a named interval from its menu, or drag on the roughness curve.
  3. Change what the tones are made of: drag the bars under What each tone is made of, or pick a preset.
  4. Or press any of the Try this buttons, which set things up and explain what to listen for.

Intervals are measured in cents: 100 cents is one piano semitone and 1200 cents is an octave (twice the frequency). Cents are logarithmic, like hearing: adding cents multiplies frequency, by 2c/1200.

Who is beating whom

How this works, and what the model leaves out

Beats. Two sine waves at frequencies f₁ and f₂ add up to sin(2πf₁t) + sin(2πf₂t) = 2 cos(π(f₁−f₂)t) · sin(π(f₁+f₂)t): a tone at the average frequency whose loudness rises and falls |f₁ − f₂| times per second. The little waves in Who is beating whom draw one second of that swell. As a rough guide, below about 10–15 beats per second you hear a wobble; faster beats fuse into a buzzy roughness; and once two partials are more than about a critical band apart (a span that is roughly a minor third in the middle of the piano, and wider at the bottom), the ear hears them as separate and smooth.

Equal temperament. Pianos split the octave into 12 equal ratios of 21/12 ≈ 1.05946, so n semitones is a ratio of 2n/12. For 0 < n < 12 that is irrational, so no piano interval except the octave is exactly a whole-number ratio, and so no piano interval lines its partials up exactly. Some come very close: the fifth is 700 cents against 701.96 for 3:2. Others are well off: the major third is 400 cents against 386.31 for 5:4, and the minor third is 300 against 315.64 for 6:5. Equal temperament makes every key equally usable by making every key slightly out of tune.

The roughness model. Plomp and Levelt (1965) asked listeners to rate how rough pairs of pure sine tones sounded. Roughness was zero at unison, peaked when the tones were about a quarter of a critical band apart, and faded away beyond about a whole critical band. William Sethares (1993) fitted a formula to their curve. This page adds that formula up over every pair of partials that belong to different tones: for partials at f and f + Δf with loudnesses a₁ and a₂, the roughness is min(a₁, a₂) · 5 · (e−3.51x − e−5.75x), where x = 0.24 Δf / (0.0207 f + 18.96). That last expression is why low notes are rougher: the ear's resolution in hertz doesn't shrink as fast as the intervals do.

What it leaves out. This is sensory roughness only, and the units are arbitrary. It says nothing about why an octave or a fifth tends to fuse into "one sound", about loudness differences across the frequency range, masking, phase, how partials change over time in real instruments (which have more than eight), or the enormous effects of musical context, training and culture on what people hear as consonant. A slow beat gets a low roughness score because it isn't rough, it's a wobble; that's why the list above shows slow beats first. The model is a good explanation of the shimmer and the dips, not a complete theory of consonance.

Stretched partials. The stretch slider puts partial n at ns times the fundamental instead of n times. Real piano strings have slightly sharp upper partials (inharmonicity), which is one reason piano tuners stretch octaves a little; the stretch here is a simple, exaggerated stand-in for that kind of thing, not a model of a piano. When the partials move, the dips move with them, to s times their usual size in cents (the purple ticks). This is Sethares's central point: which intervals sound consonant depends on the timbre, and you can design one to suit the other.

II. The Chord Clock

Music in the Western tradition uses 12 notes, and then the names repeat: go up 12 semitones from C and you are on C again, an octave higher. So the 12 notes can be drawn around a clock, each numbered by how many semitones it lies above C. If you forget which octave each note is in (musicians call what's left a pitch class), every chord becomes a shape on the clock, and shapes show things that note names hide.

  1. Click dots to add or remove notes. Play sounds them one at a time from the bottom, then together.
  2. ◀ ▶ turn the shape one step: every note moves down or up a semitone. Musicians call this transposing. It changes the chord's name but not its sound quality.
  3. Flip mirrors the shape across the vertical C–F♯ line: note number k becomes 12 − k, so every interval that went up now goes down by the same amount. (Theorists call this inversion, which is unrelated to "inverting" a chord by changing its bass note.)
  4. Show intervals draws a line between every pair of notes, coloured by the interval between them.

About the clock, and what it ignores

What's thrown away. The clock keeps only which of the 12 pitch classes are present. It forgets octaves (C E G and E G C are the same shape), which note is in the bass, how often a note is doubled, and spelling: on a piano C♯ and D♭ are the same key, but musicians write one or the other depending on the key they're in. The names on this page use one fixed spelling, so you'll see A♭ major rather than G♯ major. That loss of information is exactly why one shape can carry several chord names (C6 and Am7): in real music, the bass note and the context decide which reading is meant.

Where this comes from. Treating chords as sets of pitch classes, equivalent under transposition and inversion, is the basis of musical set theory (Allen Forte, The Structure of Atonal Music, 1973). The 12 three-note shapes in the gallery are Forte's trichords 3-1 to 3-12, and pairs of different shapes with the same interval counts are his "Z-related" pairs. Shapes that land on themselves before a full turn are what Olivier Messiaen called modes of limited transposition (the whole-tone and octatonic scales are two of his).

The counting. Burnside's lemma says that the number of different shapes equals the average, over all the moves you allow (12 rotations, or 12 rotations plus 12 flips), of the number of chords that the move leaves unchanged. A rotation by r steps splits the clock into gcd(r, 12) cycles, each 12/gcd(r, 12) long, and a chord is unchanged only if it is a union of whole cycles. A flip either passes through two opposite dots (fixing them and pairing up the other ten) or between dots (pairing up all twelve). The How many chords like this? panel shows the sum for the current number of notes.
